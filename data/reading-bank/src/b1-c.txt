title: The Ferry Ticket
STORY
Nadia stood on the harbour wall with a paper ticket in her hand. The wind pulled at her coat, and the water looked dull and grey. A long queue moved slowly toward the gate. Her ferry was due to leave at ten, and it was the only boat that morning. At the gate a worker in a blue jacket read the date on the ticket. He shook his head and handed it back. The date was for the next day, not for this morning. Nadia felt her face grow warm. She had written the number in a hurry the night before. The worker pointed to a small window beside the gate. He said she might change the ticket if one seat was still free. Nadia stepped out of the queue and walked to the window. She explained the mistake in a quiet voice. A clerk checked a screen and nodded. One seat remained on the ten o'clock ferry. Nadia paid a small fee and received a new ticket. The clerk told her to hold it carefully because the wind was strong. Nadia folded the paper and slid it into the inside pocket of her coat. She fastened the button and returned to the queue. This time the worker smiled and waved her through the gate. On board she found a seat beside a window. The crossing was calm, and Nadia watched the island grow near.

A boy knocked a crate of jam with his bag. Two jars rolled toward the path. Tomas caught one, and Rosa caught the other before it hit the stones. Nothing broke. The boy's mother said sorry and bought a jar to make up for the scare. After lunch a teacher ordered ten cards for her class. Rosa wrapped them in brown paper and tied the parcel with string. Tomas checked the coins in his tin and wrote the total in a small book. Near four o'clock the best cards were gone. He lowered the price of the last jars and called each one a bargain. An older man bought all three and said the jam smelled like summer. Tomas packed the empty crate and folded the cloth. Rosa counted the notes again while he took down the sign. They had sold more than last week. Tomas decided that a tidy stall would keep both the jam and the customers safe.

Owen said sore fingers were normal in the first week. He told her to practise for fifteen minutes and then rest. Lina agreed and wrote the plan in her notebook. Each evening she sat on a chair and played the chord slowly. She counted a steady rhythm under her breath. On Friday she could move to the next chord without a long pause. She made mistakes, but she did not stop. On Saturday her brother knocked and asked about the noise. Lina played the short tune once for him. One note was late, but the rhythm stayed even. Her brother clapped and said he could hear the song. Lina put the guitar back in the case and flexed her sore hand. She was not ready for a concert, but she could try the tune at the birthday tea.

Mateo took a photo, and they left the puzzle on the table overnight. Sofia made tea, and they talked about a harder puzzle next month. She felt tired, but the finished lake made the room feel calm. Working side by side had been better than hunting for pieces alone.
